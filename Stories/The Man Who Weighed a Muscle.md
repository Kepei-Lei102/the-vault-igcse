# The Man Who Weighed a Muscle 称量肌肉的人

> *"The language, and the letters, in which the Creator speaks in his works are geometrical figures and demonstrations."*
> — Giovanni Alfonso Borelli, dedication of *De Motu Animalium*, signed in a Roman school on 1 December 1679, thirty days before he died

## Cast of Characters

- **Giovanni Alfonso Borelli** (1608–1679) — mathematician, physician, astronomer; the son of a Spanish soldier who hid his father's name all his life. Wrote the first book to treat the human body as a set of levers.
- **Tommaso Campanella** (1568–1639) — Dominican philosopher, jailed in Naples for heresy and conspiracy against Spain; the prisoner who taught a soldier's boy.
- **Benedetto Castelli** (1578–1643) — Galileo's pupil and friend, professor in Rome; Borelli's teacher.
- **Marcello Malpighi** (1628–1694) — anatomist, later the discoverer of the capillaries; Borelli's colleague at Pisa, who turned his mind toward muscle.
- **Vincenzo Viviani** (1622–1703) — Galileo's last disciple, and Borelli's enemy at the Medici court.
- **Christina of Sweden** (1626–1689) — a queen who gave up her throne, lived in Rome, and kept a scientific academy; the patron who paid for the book.
- **Carlo Giovanni di Gesù** — head of the Piarist order, whose school sheltered Borelli; the editor who saw the book through the press.

## 中文锚点

1679 年秋天，罗马一所修会学校里住着一位七十一岁的老人。他被西西里的总督判了流放，回去就是死罪，家产也被没收，只能靠修士们的接济过日子。他手里有一部写了二十年的书，荷兰、法国和意大利好几所大学都说愿意自己出钱替他印，他却把书献给了那位在他最难的时候资助过他的女王。最后的两年里，他在这所学校里教修会的孩子们数学，一页一页把书写完。书里有一个实验：让一个结实的年轻人把手臂向前平伸，在指尖挂上重物，一直加到再也托不住，26 磅。他量出肌腱离肘关节的距离还不到手臂长度的二十分之一，于是算出，这块肌肉产生的拉力超过 560 磅。书还在印刷厂的时候，他在 1679 年的最后一夜去世，是他的学生们把书印完的。这就是博雷利：落到这般境地，他没有停下来抱怨，而是守着一个学校、一群学生和一本书，把我们身体里的杠杆一条一条算了出来。

## Act I — The soldier's son (Naples, 1608–1630)

He was born on 28 January 1608 inside a fortress. His father, Miguel Alonso, was a Spanish soldier of the garrison of the Castel Nuovo in Naples; his mother, Laura Porrello, was a local woman. The baby was baptised Giovan Francesco Antonio. He was the eldest of six children, and only he and one brother, Filippo, lived to grow up.

In 1614 the father was posted to the Castel Sant'Elmo, where one of the prisoners was Tommaso Campanella, a Dominican friar condemned for heresy and for plotting against Spanish rule. Somehow the soldier came into contact with the prisoner, and it cost him: after a secret trial he was found guilty and banished to Rome. He was pardoned and returned in 1617, and died in 1624.

Campanella, meanwhile, was moved in 1618 to the Castel Nuovo under looser conditions, where he could receive visitors and teach. The boy who grew up in that fortress very likely became one of his pupils. Some writers once guessed that Campanella was his real father; the standard Italian biography calls that "against all likelihood".

When he grew up, the boy dropped his father's name. He took **Borelli** from his mother's surname and **Alfonso** from his father's *Alonso*, and his brother did the same. All his life he blurred the date and place of his birth. He had three things to hide: a father with a conviction, a teacher in prison for heresy, and Spanish blood, in a man who came to hate Spanish rule over Italy.

## Act II — Messina and the fevers (1630–1656)

Campanella, freed in 1628, introduced him in Rome to Benedetto Castelli, Galileo's former student, who taught Euclid and Apollonius. Borelli never met Galileo, as far as anyone can show, but he learned Galileo's method from the man who had learned it at Galileo's side.

On Castelli's recommendation the city of Messina, in Sicily, gave him its chair of mathematics. He climbed Etna in 1637, joined the Accademia della Fucina (a society of young men who disliked Spanish rule), and edited the lost books of Apollonius from an old Sicilian manuscript.

Then came the fevers of 1647 and 1648, which emptied much of Sicily. Borelli the mathematician went from town to town, collected the notes of autopsies, and in 1649 published a book on the causes of the malignant fevers. Historians credit it as one of the first times anyone brought the experimental method to the study of living bodies.

## Act III — Pisa, the comet, and the enemy (1656–1667)

In 1656 the Medici gave him the chair of mathematics at Pisa. His first lectures went badly: he was long-winded and dull, and the students hissed and shouted until one day he had to stop before the end. He did not stay dull.

At Pisa he met the young anatomist Marcello Malpighi, and in 1657 both became founding members of the **Accademia del Cimento**, the Academy of Experiment, a laboratory kept by Prince Leopoldo de' Medici. It was Malpighi's dissections that turned Borelli toward muscle: how could soft meat lift a body?

He worked on the heavens too. When a comet appeared in December 1664, he showed from its parallax that it lay beyond the Moon, where the old astronomy said nothing could change. In 1666 he published a theory of the moons of Jupiter: each moon **tends toward Jupiter**, and its motion around the planet keeps it from falling in, like a stone whirled on a string. Isaac Newton owned the book, and the *Principia* cites Borelli's measurements of Jupiter's moons.

He also made an enemy. Vincenzo Viviani, Galileo's last disciple, had spent years reconstructing the same lost books of Apollonius that Borelli had translated, and persuaded the Medici to hold back Borelli's edition. The quarrel spread through the Cimento, over experiments on cannon shot and over who deserved the credit. Viviani won the court. In 1667 Borelli left Tuscany for good.

## Act IV — Exile (1667–1677)

Back in Messina, he watched Etna erupt in 1669 and wrote its history. But the city was turning against its Spanish governor, and Borelli was a friend of the rebel leaders and a member of the anti-Spanish academy. He was suspected of being the mind behind the conspiracy. In 1672 the governor banished him and others from the island, under pain of death if they returned, and confiscated their property.

He went to Rome with nothing. The exiled Queen Christina of Sweden took him in: he joined her academy and depended on her help. His biographer records one small humiliation. Borelli had once mocked astrology in print. In Rome, needing a patron, he wrote a speech in praise of it and read it aloud at the queen's academy.

By 1676 he was complaining that enemies blocked his work. One account, recorded in the Galileo Project's biographical file, says that in 1677 all his belongings were stolen, and a later retelling blames a servant; the standard Italian biography mentions only the confiscation. Either way, in September 1677, at sixty-nine, he accepted the charity of the Piarist Fathers, the Clerks Regular of the Pious Schools, at their house beside the church of San Pantaleo, near the Piazza Navona. In return he taught mathematics to their novices.

## Act V — The weighing

![[borelli-weighs-a-muscle-comic.png|700]]

*A strong young man, a cord and some iron: Proposition XXII, imagined in the Piarist schoolroom. The book does not say where or when the test was made.*

The book he had carried for twenty years was *De Motu Animalium*, *On the Motion of Animals*. Its programme is in its first lines: to give the study of animal motion "mathematical demonstrations", so that it could stand beside astronomy as a science. Four of its propositions show what that meant.

**Proposition XXII: the arm.** A strong young man holds his arm straight out, palm up. A cord hangs from his fingertips, and weights are added "until the surplus of the muscles' power vanishes entirely". The most he can hold, Borelli writes, "by the teaching of experience", is 26 pounds. The forearm and hand weigh about 4 pounds, acting at their middle, which is the same as 2 pounds at the fingertips: 28 pounds in all. Then the lever. "By diligent inquiry", the length from the elbow to the fingertips is found to be more than twenty times the distance from the elbow's pivot to the line of the tendon. So the muscles pull with more than twenty times 28 pounds: **more than 560 pounds**.

In modern units, 560 Roman pounds is about 190 kg-force, or 1.9 kN, to hold 8.8 kg. Measured today, the tendon runs about 4 cm from the elbow's axis and the fingertips sit about 44 cm out, a ratio nearer 11 than 20, so Borelli's number is about twice too large. His conclusion is exactly right: **a muscle pulls many times the weight it holds**, because it sits close to the joint and trades force for speed.

**Proposition LXI: the back.** A porter bends his back under a load of 120 pounds carried at the base of his neck. Borelli models each vertebra as a balance with its pivot at the middle of the vertebra. On one side press the load and the upper body, half of a 150-pound man less 18 pounds of guts below the waist: 57 + 120 = 177 pounds, at about seven times the vertebra's half-width in front of the pivot. On the other side pull the back muscles, less than three half-widths behind it, helped by the springy disc. The arms are in the ratio 3 : 14, so muscles and a third of the disc together must supply $177 \times \tfrac{14}{3} = 826$ pounds: **413 pounds in the muscles and 1239 in the disc** at the lowest lumbar vertebra.

That is 1.4 kN in the muscles and 4.1 kN in the disc, for a load of 41 kg. Modern models of a bent lift give forces of the same size.

**Proposition CCIV: flight.** "It is impossible that men should be able to fly artificially by their own strength." A bird's flight muscles weigh at least a sixth of its body; a man's chest muscles, "not a hundredth part". No lever can help, because a lever that multiplies force divides speed, and a wing must be fast. He dismissed, in the same breath, a floating airship lifted by globes emptied of air, a design the Jesuit Francesco Lana de Terzi had published in 1670: the globe would need to hold more than 22,000 cubic feet and have a skin so thin it could be neither made nor kept from collapsing.

**Proposition LXXIII: the heart.** Here the method ran away with him. The heart, he calculated, overcomes a resistance "greater than the force of a weight of 180,000 pounds", about 61 tonnes. The true work of one beat is about the energy of lifting a 100 g apple one metre: the left ventricle pushes some 70 millilitres against about 16 kPa, $70 \times 10^{-6} \times 16 \times 10^{3} \approx 1.1$ J. In 1717 the English physician James Keill called Borelli's figure exaggerated, and his own estimate was criticised in turn.

## Act VI — The last month (1679)

By late 1679 the book was finished and Christina had agreed to pay for printing. Its editor later wrote that universities in Holland, France and Italy had "most earnestly" asked for the work, promising to print it at their own expense, and that Borelli set them all aside for the queen's name. On the first of December 1679 he signed the dedication, "from our Pious Schools of Saint Pantaleon".

He had corrected the proofs of the first part when he fell ill. The editor calls it pleurisy; modern writers call it pneumonia. On the eighteenth day of the illness, "between the last hour of the dying year 1679 and the first of the one being born", he died, with the Piarist Fathers standing round him reading the prayers for the dying. He was buried in their church of San Pantaleo, as he had asked. The editor wrote that his body, buried among them, was more precious to the house than any inheritance; and that the inheritance itself, "most slender" as it was, they counted the greatest, as a proof of his love.

The first volume came out in 1680 and the second in 1681. The editor's preface says the book was finished by the novices Borelli had taught for two years, who had followed his mind closely, "partly copying, partly discussing, partly bringing the bricks and the sand". It also records, to his credit in their eyes, that when the lesson turned to systems of the world he would say that whatever others claimed was to be left aside: "Thus the holy Church teaches, thus it must be believed." The man who taught Galileo's method in a religious house did not teach Galileo's cosmos there.

## Honest edges

- **He hid where he came from.** The changed name, the blurred birth date and the silence about his father were deliberate. His own editor gave only his parents' names and the fortress he was born in.
- **He was hard to work with.** A search committee in Bologna found him "a trifle capricious". His biographer describes a firmness that others read as a show of superiority, and the war with Viviani was as much his temper as Viviani's.
- **He bent where he had to.** A praise of astrology for a patron, and a Copernican question closed with "thus the Church teaches". Those were the terms of his shelter, and he met them.
- **Some of his numbers are wrong.** The biceps force is about twice too large and the heart's force is wrong by a factor of thousands. He also added the forces on every vertebra of the back into totals of 25,585 and 6,404 pounds, which describe no single place in the body. The method was right before the measurements were.
- **The flight argument is right for the wrong reason.** No man flaps his way into the air. But his claim that a bird's wings must exert ten thousand times its weight is wildly high, and later writers blamed his authority for delaying experiments with heavier-than-air machines.

## Cultural ripples

*De Motu Animalium* became the handbook of the iatromechanical school, which tried to explain the body by the laws of machines. The textbook question of what force the biceps needs to hold a ball is Proposition XXII. In 1984 the American Society of Biomechanics named its highest honour the **Borelli Award**. Sanan and Rengachary's history of spinal mechanics starts with Proposition LXI, and the whole book has been in English since Paul Maquet's translation of 1989.

# The Kinetic Chain 动力链

> Sit on a chair with your feet off the floor and throw a ball as far as you can. Then stand up, take one step and throw again.
>
> It is the same arm and the same ball, and the second throw goes much farther. The arm did not get stronger in ten seconds. So where did the extra come from?

## Try it before you read on

Three experiments, each a few minutes, no equipment beyond two balls. Write the numbers down: the sections below explain each one.

1. **The seated throw.** Throw from a chair, feet off the floor, then standing with one step. Pace out both distances.
2. **The two jumps.** Stand by a wall with chalk on your fingers. First jump from a squat you have held still for three seconds. Then jump with a quick dip first. Mark both heights.
3. **The two balls.** Hold a tennis ball on top of a basketball, a little above waist height, and let go of both together. Outdoors.

## Definition

### Formal

A **kinetic chain** is a series of body segments joined at joints, through which force, momentum and energy pass on the way to the segment that does the task: the hand that throws, the foot that kicks, the fist, the racket. It is not a new law of physics. It is Newton's laws applied to a body made of linked parts, and it comes down to four statements.

1. **The ground.** The only outside forces on a body standing on the floor are its weight and the push of the floor. Every change in the motion of the body as a whole is paid for there.
2. **The order.** Large, heavy segments near the ground move first and slowly. Each hands its motion to a lighter segment further out, which moves faster. The order runs from the middle of the body to its ends.
3. **The springs.** Tendons and ligaments stretch, store energy and give it back faster than a muscle could supply it.
4. **The weak link.** If one segment passes on less than it should, the same result at the hand demands more from the smaller joints after it.

### Intuitive

A whip has no muscle in its tip, yet the tip is the fastest part. The handle moves slowly and heavily; the motion runs along a cord that gets thinner and lighter, and the same energy carried by less and less mass means more and more speed. Your body is built the same way: a 35 kg trunk, a 2 kg upper arm, a 1.5 kg forearm and hand. A good throw is a whip crack made of bone.

### 中文锚点

坐在椅子上，双脚离地，把球扔出去。不管手臂多用力，球都飞不远。站起来，迈一步再扔，同一只手臂能把球扔得远得多。手臂并没有在这几秒钟里变强，变的是这次投掷从哪里开始：从地面开始。腿蹬地，这一蹬带动髋和躯干转起来，躯干带着肩，肩带着肘，到最后一刻手才松开。身体里又大又重的部分先动，动得慢；它把自己的运动交给下一个更轻的部分，轻的部分就动得更快，就像鞭梢比鞭柄快得多。这样一节一节往下传，就是动力链。所以手肘疼，常常要回头去看髋和腰：哪一节没有把运动传下去，下一节更小的关节就得自己把缺的那部分补上，而小关节本来就不是干这个活的。

## The cast

The numbers below are for an adult of 70 kg and 1.75 m, using the standard table of segment masses and lengths (Winter, *Biomechanics and Motor Control of Human Movement*). Each moment of inertia is taken about the joint the segment turns on, treating limbs as uniform rods ([[Moment of Inertia]]).

| Segment | Share of body mass | Mass | Length | Moment of inertia about its joint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trunk | 49.7 % | 34.8 kg | | 0.40 kg m², as a cylinder of radius 0.15 m about the spine |
| Upper arm | 2.8 % | 1.96 kg | 0.33 m | 0.071 kg m² about the shoulder |
| Forearm and hand | 2.2 % | 1.54 kg | 0.36 m to a held ball | 0.085 kg m² about the elbow, ball included |
| Thigh | 10.0 % | 7.0 kg | 0.43 m | |
| Shank and foot | 6.1 % | 4.3 kg | 0.43 m | |

Read the mass column from top to bottom. From trunk to hand the mass falls by a factor of more than twenty. That taper is the whole design.

## 1. Force comes from the ground

[[Centre of Mass]] proves that internal forces cancel in pairs, so they cannot change the motion of a body's centre of mass. Muscles are internal. A muscle can pull your hand forward only by pulling something else backward. The one thing that can be pushed backward without moving is the Earth, and the only place you touch it is under your feet.

**The jump.** To rise 0.40 m you must leave the floor at $v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 0.40} = 2.80\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. For a 70 kg body that is a momentum of $70 \times 2.80 = 196\ \text{N s}$, and by the impulse rule in [[Linear Momentum]] it must come from the net force during the push. If the push lasts 0.25 s, the mean net force is $196 / 0.25 = 784\ \text{N}$ upward. The floor must therefore push with $784 + 687 = 1471\ \text{N}$, a little over twice body weight. Your legs did the work. The floor supplied the force.

**The turn.** A throw starts by turning the trunk, and a turn needs a torque from outside. The feet supply it as a **couple** ([[Torque]]): one foot pushes the ground forward, the other backward, and friction pushes back. For a trunk torque of 120 N m:

| Distance between the feet | Friction at each foot | Grip needed, $\mu$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0.40 m, feet together | 300 N | 0.87 |
| 0.80 m | 150 N | 0.44 |
| 1.30 m, a full stride | 92 N | 0.27 |

Each foot carries half the weight, 343 N, so by [[The Friction Limit]] the friction available is $\mu \times 343$ N. Feet together, the throw needs more grip than most floors give. A long stride is how a thrower buys torque from the ground. This is the answer to experiment 1: in the chair your feet touched nothing, and the chain had no first link.

## 2. The body's levers trade force for speed

Hold your forearm level with a 5 kg dumbbell in the hand. The biceps attaches to the forearm about 4 cm from the elbow; the dumbbell sits 35 cm out. Taking moments about the elbow ([[Forces and Equilibrium]]):

$$F_{\text{biceps}} \times 0.04 = (5 \times 9.81)(0.35) + (1.54 \times 9.81)(0.174) = 17.2 + 2.6 = 19.8\ \text{N m}$$

$$F_{\text{biceps}} = \frac{19.8}{0.04} = 495\ \text{N}$$

The muscle pulls with ten times the weight it holds. That looks like bad engineering until you read the lever the other way. When the biceps shortens by 1 cm, the hand moves $0.35 / 0.04 \approx 9$ cm in the same time. **The body pays in force to buy speed**, at every joint. Muscles are strong and slow; they sit close to the joint so that the far end of the bone can be fast.

## 3. Big to small, in order

### The simplest chain: two balls

Experiment 3 is a chain with two links and no muscles. Both balls reach the floor at speed $v$. The basketball bounces first and starts upward at $v$ while the tennis ball is still coming down at $v$. They collide.

*Trigger: two bodies, a short collision, nothing else pushing along the line. Tools: conservation of momentum and, for an elastic collision, the reversal of relative velocity, both from [[Linear Momentum]].*

Take up as positive, with $m_1$ the large ball. Before: $u_1 = +v$, $u_2 = -v$.

1. Relative velocity reverses: $v_2 - v_1 = u_1 - u_2 = 2v$.
2. Momentum: $m_1 v - m_2 v = m_1 v_1 + m_2 (v_1 + 2v)$.
3. Solve:

$$v_1 = \frac{m_1 - 3m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v, \qquad \boxed{\,v_2 = \frac{3m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v\,}$$

If $m_1$ is much larger than $m_2$, the small ball leaves at nearly $3v$ and rises nearly nine times as high. For a 0.62 kg basketball and a 0.058 kg tennis ball the factor is 2.66, a height ratio of 7.1. The basketball leaves at only $0.66v$: it has given up 57 % of its kinetic energy. **A heavy body slowed a little; a light body sped up a lot.** Real balls are not perfectly elastic, so expect less than 7, but far more than 1.

### The throw, measured on a model

A throw is the same hand-off with joints in between. The model behind every number in this section is a chain of three links seen from above: the trunk turning about the spine, the upper arm, and the forearm with hand and ball, using the masses in the table. Its assumptions are these.

| Assumption | Value |
|---|---|
| Strongest torque of each muscle group | trunk 120 N m, shoulder 80 N m, elbow 40 N m |
| A muscle weakens as its joint turns faster, in a straight line to zero | trunk at 15 rad s⁻¹, shoulder and elbow at 30 rad s⁻¹ |
| Each muscle group switches on once and stays on | |
| Joints have elastic end stops | 400 N m per radian past the end of range |
| The ball leaves when the elbow is 15° short of straight | |

The values are round numbers of a realistic size, not measurements of any person. The equations of motion are Newton's second law for three linked bodies, solved numerically, and the energy books balance to within 0.1 J in every run: the work the muscles do equals the kinetic energy at release.

Two throws, same body, same muscles. In the first, every muscle fires at the start. In the second, the shoulder waits 84 ms and the elbow 146 ms, the delays a search found to be best.

![[kinetic-chain-strobe.svg|760]]

| | Everything at once | In order |
|---|---|---|
| Ball speed | 12.3 m s⁻¹ (44 km/h) | 18.1 m s⁻¹ (65 km/h) |
| Time to release | 104 ms | 195 ms |
| Work by trunk, shoulder, elbow muscles | 15, 20, 24 J | 68, 49, 5 J |
| Energy of forearm, hand and ball at release | 58 J | 116 J |

Firing in order gives 1.5 times the speed and twice the energy. With muscles that do not weaken with speed, the gap widens: 14.6 against 28.5 m s⁻¹.

**Why order wins.** Fired at once, the elbow is nearly straight after 104 ms, and the throw is over when the trunk has turned 8°. In the ordered throw it turns 47°. The big muscles, which hold most of the energy, never got to spend it. A small joint is quick and has a short range. It has to wait.

### Who did the work

![[kinetic-chain-sequence.svg|760]]

Each link peaks later than the one before: the trunk at 41 ms (400° per second), the upper arm at 127 ms (1100° per second), the forearm at 193 ms (2630° per second). And each **slows down as the next speeds up**. At release the trunk and upper arm together hold 5 J. They have given almost everything away.

![[kinetic-chain-energy.svg|760]]

The elbow's own muscles did 5 J of work. The forearm, hand and ball left with 116 J. The other 111 J was made by the trunk and shoulder muscles and arrived at the forearm through the force at the elbow joint. Switch the elbow muscles off entirely and the ball still leaves at 17.9 m s⁻¹, slower by 1 %.

> [!info] The measurements agree
> In a throw the elbow straightens at about 2400° per second. The model gives 2490° per second. Measured on throwers, "work during both the cocking and acceleration phases of a normal throw is very low at the wrist and either low or negative at the elbow" (Roach and Lieberman, 2014). A man whose triceps was paralysed could still straighten his elbow rapidly in a throw (Roberts, 1971). And throwers held so that they could neither step nor turn the body reached about half their normal ball speed (Toyoshima and others, 1974).

### Timing

| Shoulder fires | 60 ms early | 20 ms early | on time | 20 ms late | 60 ms late |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ball speed, m s⁻¹ | 17.3 | 18.0 | 18.1 | 17.9 | 16.1 |

The whole throw lasts a fifth of a second. An error of 20 ms costs 1 %; an error of 60 ms costs up to 11 %. Skill in a throw is mostly the timing of the large links, which is why practice changes a throw more than arm strength does.

> [!info] Beyond syllabus — the whip
> Recall the taper in the table of segments. A whip is the limiting case: a wave travels along a cord whose mass per metre keeps falling, so the speed of the moving part keeps rising. The crack is a small sonic boom, made when the tip passes the speed of sound (Goriely and McMillen, 2002).

## 4. Springs: slow in, fast out

A muscle is limited in **power**, the rate of doing work ([[Work, Energy and Power]]). A spring is not. Stretch a spring slowly, release it quickly, and the same energy comes out in a fraction of the time: $P = E / t$ with a small $t$.

- **Running.** At 4.5 m s⁻¹ the Achilles tendon was estimated to store about 35 % of the energy lost and regained in each step, and the arch of the foot about 17 % (Ker and others, 1987). Measured directly in runners at 3.5 m s⁻¹, the Achilles tendon returns about 11 J a step (Kharazi and others, 2021). Returned over a tenth of a second, 11 J is 110 W from a strip of tendon that burns no fuel. [[Tendons as Springs]] works through the numbers.
- **Jumping.** This is experiment 2. With the same starting position, a jump with a quick dip was on average 3.4 cm higher than a jump from a held squat (Bobbert and others, 1996). The dip stretches the tendons and gives the muscles time to build force before the push begins.
- **Throwing.** In the cocked position the shoulder's ligaments and tendons are stretched like a drawn bow. Their recoil can account for about half of the work done in the final rotation of the upper arm, which can pass 9000° per second, the fastest motion the human body produces (Roach and others, 2013).

Tendon is a good spring because of its material: stiff, so it stores energy at a small stretch ([[Stress, Strain and Young Modulus]]), and nearly elastic, so little is lost as heat. The energy stored is the area under the force-extension graph, $\tfrac12 kx^2$ ([[Hooke's Law for Springs]]).

## 5. When a link does not pass it on

Weaken the trunk in the model and leave the arm as it was.

![[kinetic-chain-weak-link.svg|760]]

| Trunk torque | Ball speed, arm unchanged | Extra torque the shoulder and elbow need to restore 18.1 m s⁻¹ | Work done by the arm |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 % | 18.1 m s⁻¹ | none | 54 J |
| 80 % | 17.3 m s⁻¹ | +22 % | 67 J |
| 60 % | 16.8 m s⁻¹ | +47 % | 77 J |

The thrower has two choices: throw slower, or make the small joints work harder. Athletes choose the second. Sports medicine reports the same trade in its own numbers: a 20 % drop in the energy delivered from hip and trunk is estimated to need a 34 % rise in the turning speed of the shoulder (Kibler and Chandler, quoted in Seroyer and others, 2010). A pitcher's elbow already carries a twisting torque of about 64 N m in every throw, and the shoulder a pull of up to 81 % of body weight. There is little room for more.

**Take the ground away** and the same thing happens. On ice, with $\mu = 0.05$, the feet can supply 22 N m and no more. The model's best throw drops to 15.1 m s⁻¹, the shoulder does 63 J where it did 49 J, and the trunk ends the throw turning backward. With nothing to hold it, the arm pushes the body the other way, which is Newton's third law doing the accounting.

> [!warning] What physics can and cannot say here
> The model shows where load goes when a link weakens. It cannot examine a body. Pain that stays, swelling, or a joint that gives way needs a doctor or a physiotherapist. What the physics does give you is the right first question: not only "what hurts?" but "which link before it has stopped doing its share?"

### What this means in a gym

- **Train the order.** Exercises that start from the feet and end at the hands (a medicine-ball throw, a kettlebell swing, a push press) practise the hand-off. Exercises that isolate one joint build the engine without the timing.
- **Train the big links.** The table of work says where a throw's energy is made: 116 J of 121 J came from trunk and shoulder.
- **Keep the load close.** The back is a link that must carry torque, and torque is force times distance. Example 4 puts numbers on it.

## Worked examples

### Example 1 — how hard does the floor push in a jump?

*Trigger: a change of velocity in a known time. Tool: impulse equals change of momentum, from [[Linear Momentum]].* Worked in §1: take-off at 2.80 m s⁻¹ needs 196 N s; over 0.25 s the floor pushes with 1471 N. Halve the push time and the floor must push with $196/0.125 + 687 = 2255$ N, more than three times body weight. A quick jump is a hard one.

### Example 2 — the biceps

*Trigger: a body at rest with forces at known distances from a joint. Tool: the principle of moments, from [[Forces and Equilibrium]].* Worked in §2: 495 N to hold 5 kg. The elbow joint itself carries the difference: the bone of the upper arm pushes down on the forearm with $495 - 49 - 15 = 431$ N.

### Example 3 — three balls

A 0.62 kg basketball, a 0.058 kg tennis ball and a 0.0027 kg table-tennis ball are dropped as a stack. How fast does the top one leave, if every collision is elastic?

*Trigger: a chain of collisions, each between a heavy body moving up and a light one moving down. Tool: the boxed result of §3, applied twice, with the light ball still falling at $v$ each time.*

1. Tennis ball leaves the basketball at $v_2 = 2.66v$.
2. The tennis ball, moving up at $2.66v$, meets the table-tennis ball falling at $v$. Relative velocity reverses: $v_3 - v_2' = 2.66v + v = 3.66v$. Momentum: $0.058(2.66v) - 0.0027v = 0.058 v_2' + 0.0027(v_2' + 3.66v)$.
3. $0.1516v = 0.0607 v_2' + 0.0099v$, so $v_2' = 2.33v$ and $v_3 = 2.33v + 3.66v = 5.99v$.

Six times the speed and 36 times the height, from three links. Each heavy link slowed a little.

### Example 4 — lifting a 20 kg box

A person bends at the waist with straight legs until the trunk is level, and lifts a 20 kg box that hangs from straight arms. The body above the waist has a mass of 37.5 kg. With the trunk level, its centre of mass is 0.28 m from the joint at the base of the spine and the box is 0.45 m from it. The back muscles run 5 cm behind the joint.

*Trigger: a link held still against forces at a distance. Tool: moments about the joint.*

$$\tau = (37.5 \times 9.81)(0.28) + (20 \times 9.81)(0.45) = 103 + 88 = 191\ \text{N m}$$

$$F_{\text{muscle}} = \frac{191}{0.05} = 3.8\ \text{kN}$$

The muscles pull along the spine, so the joint is squeezed by about the same 3.8 kN: the weight of 390 kg, more than five times the person's own, on one disc. Now lift with bent knees, trunk 30° from upright, box held 0.25 m from the joint. The distances fall to $0.28 \sin 30° = 0.14$ m and 0.25 m:

$$\tau = (368)(0.14) + (196)(0.25) = 52 + 49 = 101\ \text{N m}, \qquad F_{\text{muscle}} = 2.0\ \text{kN}$$

Adding the part of the weights that now acts along the spine, $(368 + 196)\cos 30° = 0.5$ kN, the joint carries about 2.5 kN. The box did not change. The distances did. A guideline used in workplace safety puts the level for concern at 3.4 kN. The single muscle at 5 cm is a simple model; detailed models of the back give forces up to a third lower, and the comparison between the two lifts stands.

## Common Misconceptions (Teaching Notes)

### 1. "The arm throws the ball"

The arm delivers the ball. In the model 96 % of the energy that reached the forearm and ball was made by muscles above the elbow.

**Fix:** the seated throw. Nothing teaches this faster than losing half your speed by sitting down.

### 2. "A stronger arm means a faster hand"

Strength sets how much energy the muscles can make. Order and timing set how much of it reaches the hand. The same muscles gave 12.3 or 18.1 m s⁻¹.

**Fix:** compare the two columns of the table in §3. Nothing differs between them except when each muscle fired.

### 3. "Muscles move the body; the floor is just there"

Muscles move parts of the body relative to each other. Only an outside force moves the whole.

**Fix:** ask what a jump would be on a trampoline with no springs, or a throw on ice. Then read the ice run in §5.

### 4. "Tendons are ropes that join muscle to bone"

They are springs, and part of the engine. About 11 J returned every step by one tendon, measured, is energy the muscles do not have to find again.

**Fix:** experiment 2. The dip adds centimetres without adding a gram of muscle.

### 5. "The problem is where the pain is"

The overloaded joint is often the last one to be at fault. It is doing the work of a link before it.

**Fix:** the table in §5. The elbow did not change; the trunk did.
